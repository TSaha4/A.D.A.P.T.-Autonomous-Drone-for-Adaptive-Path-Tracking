\subsection{Setup and Protocol}
The original demonstration-map and fleet sweep runs used an Intel Core i5-12500H laptop with 31.7~GiB RAM under Windows~11, Python~3.11.9, NumPy~2.4.6 and OpenCV~5.0.0. New baseline, robustness and sensitivity runs used an AMD Ryzen~7 7840HS laptop with 14~GiB RAM under Linux, Python~3.14.7, NumPy~2.5.2 and OpenCV~5.0.0. Both used the evaluated pipeline defaults (Table~\ref{tab:config}). Only three substitutions are made, all outside the pipeline code. Colour sampling is performed by a deterministic \emph{simulated operator} that clicks the centroids of the $K=8$ largest connected components of strongly saturated red pixels ($H\le5$ or $H\ge175$; $S,V>180$). Display windows are suppressed and their contents captured. The mission is written to the experiment folder instead of the default output path. The three demonstration maps use the default geographic configuration, $(\varphi_0,\lambda_0)=(25.3176^\circ,82.9739^\circ)$ and $\rho=2.0$~m/px. Unless stated otherwise, results refer to the single-UAV mode; the multi-base mode is evaluated separately in Section~\ref{sec:multires}. All scripts, raw results and figure generators accompany the paper (Data Availability). The Linux Kanpur $K=4$ moderate rerun serves 35/41 drops, while the earlier Windows fleet sweep recorded 34/41; these are reported as separate platform runs, without attributing the difference to a specific cause.

\subsection{Baselines}
Perception baselines are Otsu, $k$-means, NDWI and a small U-Net on labelled S1/S2 imagery. Flood spread is compared with persistence; ordering with exact and heuristic tours; D*~Lite with A*; and fleet allocation with balancing and OR-Tools. Inputs and constraints are matched where feasible.

\subsection{Metrics}
We report flood-mask and obstacle fractions, regions and deliveries, no-path legs, route and mission size, and runtime. The labelled benchmark reports pixel-pooled IoU, F1, precision and recall plus mean chip IoU. The forecast study uses next-frame IoU against persistence. The route ablation uses exposure (route fraction over an obstacle set, sampled every 0.5~px). Fleet metrics include coverage, optimistic longest-route cruise time, route-load Gini coefficient and maximum estimated sortie battery fraction. Geographic error is geodesic distance to the supplied affine/CRS coordinates.

\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Georeferenced Flood Benchmark}
\label{sec:gtres}
We used the official Sen1Floods11 hand-labelled train/validation/test lists (251/86/90 chips before exclusions) \cite{bonafilia2020sen1floods11}. Two test chips had no finite Sentinel-1 pixels at valid labels, leaving 88. Fixed baselines include Otsu and $k$-means on Sentinel-1 VH; a dark-cluster Lab $k$-means and grayscale Otsu on Sentinel-2 RGB; and NDWI$>0$ on Sentinel-2 green/NIR \cite{mcfeeters1996ndwi}. Lab clustering was undefined on one constant-colour chip. A three-level two-band S1 U-Net was trained for 12 epochs at $128\times128$ px, selected by validation IoU (epoch 11), and evaluated at the original resolution. Cluster choices use brightness, not held-out labels.

The S2 index performs best, while unsupervised brightness clustering fares poorly. Sensor differences prevent treating the cross-sensor gaps as controlled algorithm comparisons. The production HSV operator detects \emph{red annotations}; its red-click sampler found no applicable flood marks in raw S2 RGB, so no raw-image IoU is assigned. Supplementary Fig.~S1 shows two held-out examples, including one with weak U-Net transfer.

The GeoTIFF affine and CRS map 81 locations per test chip to WGS84. With the scalar converter anchored at each chip centre and set to mean local pixel size, the median chip-maximum error is 158.5~m, the 95th percentile 406.5~m and the global maximum 419.5~m. An optional GeoTIFF input path now transforms mission pixels through the full affine and CRS. On a 512-px Ghana chip, a red rendering of the hand label produced seven drops and a 197-item mission; its navigation coordinates matched the independent affine calculation to displayed precision, versus 24.5~m maximum scalar-route error. The rendering occupies a different input domain from raw satellite imagery, and its 50.6\% HSV mask is not an independent segmentation score. Supplementary Fig.~S3 overlays the route on the co-registered Sentinel-2 image; this is a geographic product overlay, not surveyed flight accuracy.

\subsection{End-to-End Results}
\label{sec:e2e}
Table~\ref{tab:e2e} and Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline-pair} summarise the end-to-end runs. All four runs complete and produce missions that pass the validator with no errors or warnings. Path planning accounts for more than 99\% of runtime; every other stage takes less than 0.1~s. The fourth run is a label-rendered georeferenced diagnostic rather than an independent flood-detection evaluation. Three findings qualify these outcomes.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{No-path legs.} Half of the Varanasi legs (8 of 16) and 6 of 33 Kanpur legs have no path on the planning grid. The mission therefore flies straight segments across obstacles on these legs (red segments in Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline-pair}). The independent A* baseline (Section~\ref{sec:baselines}) confirms that these endpoints are genuinely disconnected on the grid: after snapping, they lie in free pockets enclosed by the inflated flood mask.
\item \emph{Segmentation failure on the thematic map.} For Assam, the sampled patches include white background, $\ell_S$ falls to $0$ and achromatic pixels pass the red hue test (Section~\ref{sec:method}-B). Of the image, 83.1\% is labelled flood, the whole map becomes a single region, and both endpoints of the only (zero-length) leg remain inside obstacles. The resulting 10-item mission is structurally valid but meaningless.
\item \emph{Geography.} All missions lie within about 2~km of the default reference, irrespective of the map content (Section~\ref{sec:geovalid}).
\end{enumerate}
An optional excessive-mask guard at 80\% stops Assam before export (exit status 2), while Varanasi and Kanpur still produce missions. The threshold is a safety diagnostic, not a calibrated classifier; Supplementary Fig.~S6 shows the failed mask and guard outcome.

\subsection{Ablation: Predicted-Flood Obstacles}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} compares planning on $O=M\cup\hat M$ with planning on $M$ alone. Predicted-only pixels cover 7.7\% (Varanasi) and 14.0\% (Kanpur) of the image. Without predicted obstacles, 33.8\% and 47.9\% of the route length lies over predicted-only area. With them, exposure falls to 17.9\% and 11.6\%. Exposure does not reach zero because the no-path legs fly straight. The same inflation, however, causes \emph{all} no-path legs: there are none with $M$ alone, against 8 and 6 with $O$. It also increases planning time by a factor of 2.2--2.9, and for Varanasi it slightly increases exposure to the current flood (3.4\% to 4.8\%). The prediction stage is therefore not uniformly beneficial: in its current form, the benefit it is designed for comes with a loss of planner completeness.

\subsection{Observed Flood Progression and Operator Robustness}
Two successive delineation pairs for one Copernicus EMSR504 Australian flood area were rasterised on a common 30~m grid and compared with persistence and the project's weather-driven dilation \cite{cems_emsr504}. Retrospective hourly precipitation and wind at the first acquisition came from the Open-Meteo historical reanalysis \cite{openmeteo_archive}. The 19--21 March 2021 pair has 2-day observed-mask IoU 0.803 for both persistence and the proxy; the 21--22 March pair has IoU 0.946 for both. At 0 and 0.4~mm/h precipitation respectively, the proxy's integer spread threshold generated no change, while mapped flood cells changed. Thus these two observed comparisons provide no evidence of forecast improvement. The reanalysis is retrospective and mapping products have observation uncertainty.
Supplementary Fig.~S2 shows the two observed transitions, the unchanged proxy and their disagreements.

To sample the interactive stage, we ran ten deterministic simulated operators per map under calm, moderate and heavy weather, varying clicks from four to eight and perturbing click locations by at most 3~px (90 full runs). The Varanasi and Kanpur mask fractions were 8.55$\pm$0.63\% and 14.56$\pm$1.19\% (mean$\pm$sample SD). Moderate weather produced no no-path legs; heavy weather produced 8.2$\pm$0.6 and 6.0$\pm$0.5 respectively. Kanpur route length was 3495$\pm$239, 3637$\pm$266 and 6580$\pm$504 display px under calm, moderate and heavy conditions. The Assam mask remained 83.09\% and yielded a structurally valid but unusable mission in every run. These are simulated click perturbations, not measurements of human variability. Supplementary Fig.~S5 shows route-length distributions by weather.

\subsection{Sensitivity}
A deterministic sweep varies the colour margins and number of operator clicks. The hue margin $\delta_H\in\{10,20,30\}$ has \emph{no} effect on any map, because the red-wrap rule replaces the hue interval. The saturation/value margin $\delta_{SV}\in\{15,30,45\}$ has a moderate effect on the two dot maps, while the Assam result is insensitive to every setting: its contamination is structural rather than a tuning artefact. Using 16 instead of 8 clicks raises the Varanasi flood fraction from 8.1\% to 9.7\% and the region count from 16 to 23, so the interactive step is a material source of variability. The ground scale $\rho$ affects only the geodetic conversion: the Varanasi route length scales linearly at 2.83~km per m/px, so the 5.66~km mission at $\rho=2$~m/px would be 263~km at the landmark-estimated scale of the map (Section~\ref{sec:geovalid}).



\subsection{Route Ordering and Planner Baseline}
\label{sec:baselines}
Two evaluation-only baselines were tested; neither changes \adapt{}. Against exact optima from Held--Karp dynamic programming \cite{held1962dynamic} on uniformly random instances, the nearest-neighbour order is on average 4.6\% (5 points) to 12.0\% (12 points) longer, with a worst case of 44.5\%. On the 16 Varanasi drop points it is 3.2\% longer than optimal. For the 33 Kanpur points only a minimum-spanning-tree lower bound is available, which limits the gap to at most 37.8\%. On the same actual points, evaluation-only 2-opt shortens the 33-point Kanpur closed Euclidean tour from 2934 to 2458 display px (16.2\%); Or-opt and an OR-Tools tour give the same length, without proving optimality. On Varanasi, Or-opt and OR-Tools reach the Held--Karp optimum of 1669 px versus nearest neighbour at 1722 px. These ordering lengths exclude obstacle detours.

A plain grid A* \cite{hart1968formal} agrees with D*~Lite on reachability for all 49 non-trivial legs and on path cost to within $2\times10^{-13}$. The evaluated D*~Lite runs are 61--112 times slower (12.67 versus 0.21~s on Varanasi; 30.83 versus 0.28~s on Kanpur) because each leg rebuilds the search instance instead of exploiting incremental reuse.



\subsection{One Drone vs.\ a Drone Fleet (Single- vs.\ Multi-UAV Planning)}
\label{sec:multires}
\emph{Protocol.} The multi-base mode (Section~\ref{sec:multi}) was run on the three maps with the simulated operator, under the dummy weather and under a moderate preset (3~mm, 12~km/h, $270^\circ$), with base caps $K\in\{1,2,3,4\}$ (24 runs). For a given map and weather, all caps share the drop points, the obstacles and the vehicle model, so $K$ is the only variable. $K=1$ is the single-UAV case, in which one vehicle flies all sorties from one base. All metrics are read from each run's own outputs, and every exported mission was re-checked by the independent validator against its route file. The \emph{estimated completion time} is the routed length of the longest mission divided by $v=5$~m/s. It assumes that all vehicles launch together and excludes take-off, landing, drop manoeuvres and reload turnaround, so it is a lower bound rather than a prediction of mission time. Table~\ref{tab:multiuav} gives the results.

\input{figures/tab_multi_uav}

\emph{Feasibility and safety checks.} Of the 24 runs, 20 produced missions, 43 in total. In these runs:
\begin{itemize}
\item all 43 missions pass the validator with no errors;
\item no two routes of a run cross, so no repair was needed;
\item no leg was flown as a straight fallback, and no route pixel lies in the detected flood;
\item every visited drop lies in its own base's territory;
\item the largest routed sortie uses 79.6\% of the battery, against the 80\% limit.
\end{itemize}
The four Assam runs under moderate weather end with status~2, because no base site reaches any drop. Under dummy weather, Assam has one base and visits 1 of 4 drops for every $K$. Both outcomes follow from the segmentation failure of Section~\ref{sec:e2e}.

\emph{Coverage.} A base can only serve drops within one single-drop sortie~\eqref{eq:reach}. That is about 832~m of routed distance in calm air, 768~m under the moderate wind and 690~m under the dummy wind, or roughly 300 and 270 display pixels at the Varanasi scale. Adding bases therefore mainly adds coverage. From $K=1$ to $K=4$, the visited drops rise as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item under dummy weather, from 6 to 12 of 22 (Varanasi) and from 10 to 23 of 41 (Kanpur);
\item under moderate weather, from 10 to 18 and from 17 to 34.
\end{itemize}
Every unvisited drop is reported. In all but two runs, the reason is that no admissible base reaches it within the cap; in two Kanpur runs, the sortie planner rejected one assigned drop. The stronger dummy wind shortens the reach and lowers coverage at every $K$. Because the visited sets differ, total route length does not compare efficiency like for like. It grows with $K$ because more drops are served and each base flies its own return legs.

\emph{Clustering.} Across the 12 multi-drone runs, 190 of 206 served regions were assigned to one drone; 16 large regions were shared by two.\par

\emph{Workload.} The nearest-feasible assignment does not balance work. On Kanpur under moderate weather, the drops per UAV are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item $K=1$: 17;
\item $K=2$: 17 and 8;
\item $K=3$: 17, 7 and 6;
\item $K=4$: 15, 7, 6 and 6.
\end{itemize}
The longest mission therefore stays at 11.0~km, and the completion estimate at 37~min, up to $K=3$; the estimate falls to 28~min only at $K=4$. On Varanasi under moderate weather, the longest mission falls from 10.3 to 6.4~km and the completion estimate from 34 to 21~min, using three of the four permitted bases.

\emph{Choosing an approach.} The planner uses one base whenever it reaches every reachable drop; otherwise adding bases raises coverage. The single-UAV approach visits all 16 Varanasi and 33 Kanpur regions but has no endurance check. Its routes of 5.66 and 18.78~km exceed the fleet model's approximate 2.1~km per-battery cruise allowance. Thus $K=1$ in the fleet mode is the battery-constrained one-drone comparison.

\emph{Scale dependence.} These coverage figures hold only for the configured scale. The $K=4$, moderate-weather runs were repeated at the landmark-estimated scales of Section~\ref{sec:geovalid} (93 and 130~m/px). At those scales a single-drop sortie spans only 6.4 and 4.5 display pixels, every drop is unreachable, and both runs end with status~2.

\emph{Runtime.} A feasible run took between about 10~s and 3~min on the evaluation laptop. These are single measurements, taken with several runs executing in parallel.

\emph{Integration equivalence.} Three identical-input reference comparisons (both maps in calm conditions and Varanasi with moderate weather) reproduced base positions, assignments, routes and drop/reload indices exactly. The reference rejects the Assam mask before planning.

\subsection{Geospatial and Mission Verification}
\label{sec:verres}
Table~\ref{tab:verif} summarises the verification results. Every result in this subsection concerns \emph{software correctness}, that is, whether the code computes what its model specifies. None of it shows that the generated coordinates match real places.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Synthetic ground truth.} On a $1000\times1000$~px synthetic image at 10~m/px, the implementation matches an independent re-derivation of \eqref{eq:enu}--\eqref{eq:geo} to within $1.4\times10^{-14}$~degrees at nine control points. Against the WGS84 geodesic ground truth, the largest error is 25.9~m at the image corners, dominated by the 0.49\% north--south scale error of $L$ at $25^\circ$, and every point is within the derived bound.
\item \emph{Round trips and edge cases.} Of 100\,000 randomised round trips through a test-only inverse, covering mid-latitudes, near-polar and near-antimeridian references, image sizes and scales from 0.05 to 633~m/px, 98\,789 are representable and agree within $6.8\times10^{-8}$~px; the remaining 1\,211 are correctly rejected.
\item \emph{Mission correspondence.} Every row of the three missions (1\,519 rows in total) matches an independent conversion of its source pixel within $3.6\times10^{-15}$~degrees. Traced back to the original image, the deviation is at most 0.57~m, entirely north--south and explained by the rounding of $H'$.
\item \emph{Resize consistency.} A synthetic three-region map run at nine display widths (300 to 2400~px, odd and even) yields drops at the same physical corners. They deviate from the model by at most one display pixel, and by $7\times10^{-10}$~m when no resizing occurs.
\item \emph{Mutation testing.} 31 of 33 injected defects are detected by the 231-test suite. Of the 24 defects in the geospatial and mission layers, 22 are detected; all 9 defects injected into the multi-base integration are detected, for example an uncompensated display scale, a disabled crossing gate, a missing reload stop or a stale reach set after nudging. The two surviving mutants are behaviour-equivalent: one removes a redundant finiteness guard, the other repeats a conversion.
\item \emph{Adversarial testing.} Property-based tests of D*~Lite and crossing checks, about 110 pathological end-to-end runs and 500 seeded fleet scenarios exposed ten defects, all corrected before the reported evaluation. None changes the missions reported above.

None of the corrections changes a mission generated in the experiments above.
\end{itemize}
During verification, the procedure exposed and led to the correction of defects in the original implementation: scale was not compensated after resizing, servo parameters were in the wrong slots, the region chosen as HOME received no delivery, the reference pixel was off by half a pixel, and invalid or polar inputs silently produced invalid coordinates. All results reported here use the corrected code.

\subsection{Geographic Validity of the Supplied Maps}
\label{sec:geovalid}
To test whether the configured geography could describe the maps, a similarity transform was fitted between visible place-name labels and approximate public coordinates of those towns. This was done for 5, 10 and 12 labels on the Varanasi, Kanpur and Assam maps; the label positions were read off by eye. The estimate is a plausibility check, not a georeference. Residuals are 15--26~px and the rotations are $-0.5^\circ$, $-1.0^\circ$ and $-4.8^\circ$. The fitted scales are about 93, 130 and 633~m/px, which is 46, 65 and 317 times the configured 2.0~m/px. The default reference lies 23~km, 319~km and about 1\,000~km from the estimated image centres. At those extents, the flat-earth model itself would err by up to 345~m, 563~m and 7.7~km (from the WGS84 oracle), and the maps' web-Mercator basemaps violate the uniform-scale assumption \cite{battersby2014implications}. The generated missions are therefore \emph{not} geographically meaningful for these maps. The cause lies in the inputs and configuration, not in the conversion code.

\subsection{Mission Semantics}
The command parameters were audited against the MAVLink definitions and the ArduPilot and PX4 documentation \cite{mavlink_common,mavlink_mission,ardupilot_missioncmds,px4_missions}; the findings are given in Section~\ref{sec:method}-K and Section~\ref{sec:limits}. The lower block of Table~\ref{tab:verif} separates measured acceptance from remaining operational checks. The multi-base reload LAND/TAKEOFF semantics are evaluated in the short ArduCopter SITL run of Section~\ref{sec:sim}: LAND disarms and resets the mission index, so automatic continuation is unsupported by this configuration \cite{ardupilot_continue}. The optional GeoTIFF route coordinates are independently recomputed from the reference affine transform and CRS; the Ghana mission has zero observed navigation-coordinate discrepancy at WPL precision.

\subsection{Simulation and Mission Execution}
\label{sec:sim}
ArduCopter~4.7.1 SITL accepted all 364 items of the exported Varanasi WPL via the MAVLink mission protocol (364 item requests; acceptance acknowledgement), and accepted a 17-item two-drop/reload WPL from the same exporter \cite{mavlink_mission,ardupilot_missioncmds}. MAVProxy independently loaded the 364-item WPL, received an accepted MISSION\_ACK, reported 364/364 items present, and downloaded 364 items with the same command sequence; its six-decimal coordinate export differed by at most $5.98\times10^{-7}$ degrees from the input. Supplementary Fig.~S7 is an excerpt from that actual ground-station transcript. The short WPL was executed in AUTO at fivefold simulated speed. Telemetry recorded arrival at 9.98~m relative altitude and servo~9 changing from 1000 to 2000~$\mu$s at the first drop; the intermediate LAND disarmed the vehicle. ArduCopter reset its mission index to TAKEOFF item~1 rather than continuing automatically at item~8. A guided rearm alone repeated the first sortie. Explicitly selecting item~8 after rearm reached the second drop; RTL landed the vehicle and the final LAND item became current, without a recorded completion event. The second DO\_SET\_SERVO command was reached, but servo~9 remained at 2000~$\mu$s because the exporter emitted no reset between drops. Thus a second release is not established. The SITL battery-voltage reading remained at 12.6~V and cannot calibrate the energy model. These outcomes come from telemetry, not WPL structure. The full 364-item route was uploaded but not flown. This is one simulated vehicle, with neither a QGroundControl/Mission Planner graphical-interface test nor a physical flight. SITL does not validate the map geography, landing-site safety or payload release mechanism.
